Not every crosswalk needs a traffic light

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE

When a crossing feels dangerous, the first request is usually a traffic light. Signals have their place, but they are expensive, they need to meet federal and state criteria, and they are not always the safest answer. Cities have a whole toolbox for crossings without a light.

Bright, wide stripes. High visibility crosswalk markings help drivers see the crossing from much farther away than two thin lines.

A yield line before the crosswalk. A row of white triangles tells drivers where to stop, which keeps a stopped car from hiding you from the next lane over.

Curb extensions. Bringing the curb out into the parking lane makes you more visible and shortens the distance you have to walk.

An island in the middle. A refuge island lets you cross one direction of traffic at a time. On a wide street that changes everything.

Push button flashing beacons. Bright yellow lights that flash when you press the button get far more drivers to stop than signs alone.

Raised crosswalks. A crosswalk at sidewalk height works like a wide speed bump. Drivers slow down, and you stay level the whole way across.

Why the street matters. The right mix depends on how wide and how fast the street is. On a quiet two lane street, good markings and curb extensions may be enough. On a wide, fast street, paint alone is not enough, and the crossing needs several of these working together.

What you can do: if a crossing near you feels scary, report it through your city's 311 system, and be specific about the location and the time of day. If your city has a transportation commission, bring it to the next meeting. A clear, calm description of a real place gives staff something they can check and act on.

What does a flashing yellow arrow mean?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

A flashing yellow arrow means you may turn, but you do not have the right of way. You must yield to oncoming traffic and to people in the crosswalk, then turn when there is a safe gap. A solid green arrow means the turn is protected, and a solid yellow arrow means the turn is about to end.

What the rule book says

The MUTCD, the national standard for signals, says traffic facing a flashing yellow arrow may cautiously enter the intersection to make the turn shown. Drivers must yield to people lawfully in the crosswalk and to vehicles lawfully in the intersection, and a driver turning left must also yield to oncoming vehicles close enough to be an immediate hazard. In plain words: it is a yield, not a go.

Left turn phasing in plain words

  • Protected: a solid green arrow. Oncoming traffic is stopped and the turn is yours.
  • Permissive: you may turn after yielding. This is what the flashing yellow arrow shows.
  • Protected and permissive: the same signal head gives a green arrow for part of the cycle and a flashing yellow arrow for another part.

Why cities switched

For decades, permissive left turns were shown with a solid green ball. Engineers worried that some drivers read it as a protected turn. A national research effort, NCHRP Project 3-54, studied the options for about seven years and was published in 2003 as NCHRP Report 493.

FHWA summarized the findings when it gave interim approval for the flashing yellow arrow on March 20, 2006: it was the best overall alternative to the circular green for permissive left turns, drivers understood it well, and it had a lower fail critical rate than the circular green. The arrow then entered the 2009 edition of the MUTCD as an alternative to the circular green.

Why it can change by time of day

A separate flashing yellow arrow signal head lets an agency run a turn as protected at rush hour and permissive late at night without changing hardware. FHWA also notes it avoids the "yellow trap," a situation with some permissive turns where a driver can be caught in the intersection thinking oncoming traffic is also stopping.

From the engineering side, that flexibility is the real win. We can match the left turn to the traffic and the crash history at each time of day instead of choosing one setting forever.

How to drive it

  • Pull forward only as far as you can wait safely, and keep your wheels straight.
  • Look for people in the crosswalk you are turning into, not just oncoming cars.
  • If the gap is not clearly safe, wait. A protected arrow or the next cycle will come.

What you can do

If a permissive left turn near you feels unsafe, report it through 311 or your city's transportation department and ask for a review of the left turn phasing at that intersection. Note the time of day and what you see, like long queues or near misses with people walking.

Frequently asked questions

Is a flashing yellow arrow a yield?

Yes. Under the MUTCD you may turn, but you must yield to oncoming vehicles close enough to be a hazard, to vehicles already in the intersection, and to people lawfully in the crosswalk.

Why did cities switch to flashing yellow arrows?

Research published as NCHRP Report 493 in 2003 found the flashing yellow arrow was the best alternative to the circular green for permissive left turns, with high driver understanding and a lower fail critical rate. FHWA allowed it by interim approval in 2006 and it entered the 2009 MUTCD.

What is left turn phasing?

It is how a signal handles left turns: protected (green arrow, oncoming traffic stopped), permissive (turn after yielding, often a flashing yellow arrow), or a mix of both in the same cycle. Agencies can change the mode by time of day.

Do I have to yield to pedestrians on a flashing yellow arrow?

Yes. The MUTCD says drivers facing a flashing yellow arrow must yield to pedestrians lawfully within the associated crosswalk.

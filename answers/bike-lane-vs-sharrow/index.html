What is a sharrow, and is it a bike lane?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

No. A bike lane is space on the road set aside for people on bikes. A sharrow, short for shared lane marking, is painted inside a regular travel lane to show where people on bikes should ride while sharing that lane with cars. It does not create bike only space. Sharrows were added to the national MUTCD, the rule book for traffic markings, in December 2009.

What sharrows are meant to do

The 2009 MUTCD, which first added the marking, lists five uses:

  • Help riders stay out of the door zone of parked cars.
  • Help riders position themselves in lanes too narrow for a car and a bike side by side.
  • Show everyone where people on bikes are likely to ride.
  • Encourage drivers to pass safely.
  • Reduce wrong way riding.

The placement rules

That same edition said sharrows should not be used where the speed limit is above 35 mph, and must not be placed in bike lanes or on shoulders. With parallel parking, the center of the marking should be at least 11 feet from the curb. Without parking, on an outside lane narrower than 14 feet, at least 4 feet. Markings go right after an intersection and then no more than 250 feet apart.

Where they help most

The Cornell Local Roads Program, citing Ithaca, New York's experience, suggests using only as many as needed and lists four good uses: wayfinding through a confusing route, the downhill side of a street that has an uphill bike lane only, specific spots where riders need a reminder of where to ride, and streets where a bike lane is wanted but not yet feasible. Ithaca installed 78 sharrows in 2009 at about $250 each, and estimated about $100 a year per marking to maintain.

My take

My engineering view, and the point of the video above: sharrows can make sense on low volume, low speed streets. On a busy street, paint in the travel lane does not do the job of a bike lane. If you want separation, see protected bike lanes.

Frequently asked questions

Is a sharrow a bike lane?

No. A sharrow marks a shared travel lane and shows where people on bikes should ride. A bike lane is space set aside for bikes.

Do drivers have to stay off sharrows?

No. A sharrow is in a lane shared by cars and bikes. It alerts drivers to where riders are likely to be and encourages safe passing.

Where should sharrows be used?

The 2009 MUTCD said not on roads with speed limits above 35 mph. Guidance from the Cornell Local Roads Program points to wayfinding, short gaps, and streets where a bike lane is not yet feasible.

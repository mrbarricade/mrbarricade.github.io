Why is right turn on red allowed, and why are cities banning it?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Right turn on red spread across the United States in the 1970s as a fuel saving measure, after the 1975 Energy Policy and Conservation Act tied federal energy assistance to allowing it. Cities are now restricting it because the turn asks drivers to scan for cars coming from one direction and people crossing from the other at the same moment.

How it became the norm

Right turn on red was once mostly a California and western states practice. During the 1970s oil crisis, states adopted it to reduce idling, and Congress sped that up with the 1975 Energy Policy and Conservation Act, which tied eligibility for federal energy assistance to allowing it. By the end of the decade, nearly every state allowed it. New York City is the well known exception. Seattle's transportation department notes the federal estimate at the time was a travel time savings of 1 to 5 seconds per turning vehicle.

The safety tradeoff

Seattle's 2023 policy memo summarizes the concern: studies after the 1975 law found right turn on red crashes with people walking increased by 60 to 70 percent, and an Institute of Transportation Engineers analysis found No Turn on Red signs can reduce pedestrian and vehicle conflicts by 92 percent during the red interval. CNN also reported on a 1982 study that found crashes rose by more than 20 percent in states that adopted the rule.

From the engineering side, the problem is attention. A driver waiting to turn right on red is looking left for a gap in traffic. The person stepping off the curb is often coming from the right.

Why cities are restricting it now

CNN reported in 2024 that Atlanta, Denver, Indianapolis, Washington, DC, Raleigh, and other cities had recently proposed or passed limits, either in busy downtowns or citywide, as pedestrian deaths reached their highest level in more than 40 years.

When engineers post No Turn on Red

The MUTCD says a No Turn on Red sign should be considered when an engineering study finds conditions like these:

  • Poor sight distance to approaching traffic.
  • An exclusive pedestrian or bicycle phase.
  • Too many conflicts with people walking, especially children, older adults, or people with disabilities.
  • More than three right turn on red crashes in 12 months on one approach.
  • A skewed intersection angle that makes it hard to see traffic.

Signs can also apply only at certain times, using blank out signs or posted hours.

What you can do

If right turns on red feel dangerous at a crossing you use, report it through 311 or your city's transportation department. Ask for a review of No Turn on Red at that intersection, and describe who is crossing and when.

Frequently asked questions

Is right on red legal everywhere?

It is allowed in most of the United States unless a sign prohibits it, but there are exceptions. New York City generally does not allow it, and a growing number of cities restrict it in busy areas. Always follow the posted signs.

Why do some signs say No Turn on Red?

An engineer found a reason in a study, such as poor sight distance, an exclusive pedestrian or bike phase, heavy pedestrian conflicts, a skewed intersection, or more than three right turn on red crashes in a year on that approach. These are the conditions the MUTCD lists.

Does banning right on red cause traffic?

The time savings was always small. When the rule spread in the 1970s, the federal estimate was 1 to 5 seconds per turning vehicle, according to Seattle's transportation department.

How do you get speed bumps on your street?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Ask your city's transportation department about its traffic calming or neighborhood traffic program, usually through 311. Staff typically measure speeds and volumes and check whether the street qualifies. On public streets you will usually get a speed hump, speed cushion, or speed table, not the short, sharp speed bump you see in parking lots.

Speed bump or speed hump?

They are not the same thing. FHWA's traffic calming guide describes a speed hump as about 12 feet long in the direction of travel and 3 to 4 inches high, built for public roads. A speed bump is much shorter, 1 to 2 feet long, can be up to 6 inches high, and belongs in parking lots and driveways, not on public streets.

Two cousins matter too. A speed cushion is a hump with gaps so wide vehicles like fire trucks can straddle it. A speed table has a flat top about 10 feet long, and when it carries a crosswalk it becomes a raised crosswalk.

Which streets qualify

Cities set their own rules, but FHWA summarizes common guidance from the Institute of Transportation Engineers and state agencies:

  • Posted speed limit of 30 mph or less.
  • Generally not where the 85th percentile speed is 45 mph or more.
  • Relatively low traffic volumes, with example caps of 3,500 to 4,000 vehicles a day.
  • Grades of 8 percent or less, and not on sharp curves.
  • Generally not on primary emergency routes or busy bus routes, where cushions are often used instead.

Do they work?

FHWA reports that a single hump slows drivers to about 15 to 20 mph as they cross it, and the effect fades with distance, so humps are usually installed in a series 260 to 500 feet apart. Field studies of speed humps measured 85th percentile speed reductions of 6 to 13 mph and crash reductions of 33 to 48 percent. Installed as a series, they typically reduce traffic volume on the street by about 20 percent.

What about fire trucks and ambulances?

This is the most common objection, and it is a fair one. FHWA reports typical delay at a speed hump of 3 to 5 seconds for a fire truck and up to 10 seconds for an ambulance carrying a patient. That is why emergency routes usually get speed cushions, which let wide vehicles pass with little or no slowdown.

What you can do

  • Search your city's site for "traffic calming" and follow its request steps, or submit a request through 311.
  • Describe the problem with specifics: time of day, the kind of speeding you see, kids or seniors walking.
  • Ask for a speed and volume study and how the results will be shared.
  • Talk with neighbors early. Calming changes a whole block, so shared support helps a request move.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a speed bump and a speed hump?

A speed hump is about 12 feet long and 3 to 4 inches high and is designed for public streets. A speed bump is 1 to 2 feet long, can be up to 6 inches high, and is meant for parking lots and driveways.

What is traffic calming?

Traffic calming is the set of physical street changes used to slow drivers, such as speed humps, speed cushions, speed tables, raised crosswalks, curb extensions, chokers, and mini roundabouts.

Do speed humps slow emergency vehicles?

Somewhat. FHWA reports typical delays of 3 to 5 seconds for a fire truck and up to 10 seconds for an ambulance with a patient. Speed cushions, which have gaps for wide vehicles, are often used on emergency routes instead.

Who decides the speed limit on a road?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Speed limits are set by state law and by the agency that owns the road. Many streets use a default limit written into state law, and the rest come from an engineering study of how fast people drive, crash history, and who uses the street. In California, the 85th percentile speed still matters, but AB 43, signed in 2021, gave cities more room to lower limits.

Default limits and studied limits

FHWA notes that posted speed limits are often the same as the statutory limit in state law. When an agency sets a different limit, the MUTCD requires an engineering study that considers the roadway context. For example, in California, residence districts have a 25 mph default limit under state law.

The 85th percentile rule

The 85th percentile speed is the speed that 15 percent of drivers exceed. For decades, many states set limits at or near it. The 11th Edition of the MUTCD, published in December 2023, changed the emphasis. It lists factors an engineering study should consider, including land use, transit, people walking and biking, road design, crash history, and the speed distribution. It also says that on urban and suburban arterials, the 85th percentile speed should not be used to set limits without considering all of those factors.

What California's AB 43 changed

AB 43 was approved by the Governor on October 8, 2021, and its Vehicle Code changes began January 1, 2022. Together with later bills, it lets California agencies:

  • Round the 85th percentile speed down to the nearest 5 mph instead of up.
  • Take an extra 5 mph off on designated safety corridors, which cannot exceed one fifth of a city's streets, or near places that draw many people walking and biking.
  • Keep the current limit, or restore the previous one, when a new survey would push it higher and no travel lanes were added.
  • Set 20 or 25 mph limits in business activity districts, like downtowns, by ordinance.

Why speed matters so much

FHWA reports that a person hit by a driver going 30 mph has a 45 percent chance of being killed or seriously injured. At 20 mph, that drops to 5 percent. FHWA also reports that after Seattle set 20 mph limits on non arterial streets and 25 mph on 200 miles of arterials as part of a broader program, traffic deaths fell 26 percent.

From my side of the table: a sign alone rarely does the whole job. Lower limits work best alongside street design that makes the slower speed feel natural.

What you can do

  • Ask your city's transportation department, through 311 or a traffic safety request, when the street's speed survey was last done and request a new one.
  • Describe who uses the street: a school, a senior center, a park, a bus stop. Those details can matter in the study.
  • Ask whether the street could qualify for a reduction under your state's rules, and whether traffic calming is an option.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 85th percentile rule?

It is the practice of setting a speed limit at or near the speed that 85 percent of free flowing drivers do not exceed, meaning 15 percent go faster. The current MUTCD says it should not be used alone on urban and suburban arterials.

How do I request a lower speed limit?

Contact your city or county transportation department through 311 or a traffic safety request, ask for a new engineering and traffic survey, and describe who uses the street. In California, ask whether AB 43 options like safety corridors or business activity districts apply.

Why are speed limits so low?

Many residential limits are defaults written into state law, such as 25 mph in residence districts in California. Speed has a large effect on injury: FHWA reports a 45 percent chance of death or serious injury for a person hit at 30 mph versus 5 percent at 20 mph.

Does lowering the speed limit actually slow drivers?

FHWA says a growing body of research shows speed limit changes alone can lead to measurable declines in speeds and crashes, and agencies often pair them with traffic calming, street design, and speed safety cameras.

How do you get a crosswalk installed on your street?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

You request it from the agency that owns the street, usually your city or county transportation or public works department, through 311 or a traffic safety request. Staff then study the spot: how many people cross, how busy and fast the traffic is, and how well drivers can see. If a crossing makes sense, the engineer picks the right treatment, which can be bright markings and signs, a refuge island, flashing beacons, or a combination.

Who actually decides

The agency that owns the road makes the call. On most neighborhood streets that is the city or county traffic engineer. On a state highway that runs through town, it is the state department of transportation, even if the city requests the change.

Every agency follows the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices, or MUTCD. The Federal Highway Administration published the 11th Edition in December 2023, and the current version is the 11th Edition with Revision 1, dated December 2025. Crosswalk markings live in its Chapter 3C. States can publish their own version, and for example, California uses the California MUTCD.

What the engineer looks at

A crosswalk request usually turns into a short engineering study. Staff look at how many people cross and when, how many lanes there are, traffic volume and speed, sight lines, crash history, and what is nearby, like a school, park, or bus stop.

Road width and traffic matter a lot. FHWA says that on multilane roads carrying more than 10,000 vehicles a day, a marked crosswalk alone is typically not sufficient, and more substantial improvements are needed. That is why the answer is sometimes "not paint alone" rather than "no."

Paint is only the start

FHWA lists several crossing tools as Proven Safety Countermeasures, with these reported benefits:

  • High visibility crosswalks can reduce pedestrian injury crashes by up to 40 percent.
  • Advance yield or stop markings and signs can reduce pedestrian crashes by up to 25 percent.
  • Intersection lighting can reduce pedestrian crashes by up to 42 percent.
  • Pedestrian refuge islands are associated with a 56 percent reduction in pedestrian crashes.
  • Rectangular rapid flashing beacons can reduce pedestrian crashes by up to 47 percent, with driver yielding rates as high as 98 percent at some sites.

As an engineer, I think of these as layers. A quiet two lane street may only need good markings. A wide, busy street needs several layers working together.

Why there might not be a crosswalk yet

Usually it is one of three things: nobody has asked and the location has never been studied, the counts did not show much crossing demand, or the street needs a bigger fix than paint and that fix needs funding. Each of those can change with a clear, specific request.

What you can do

  • Submit a request through 311 or your city's transportation or public works department. Name the exact location.
  • Describe who crosses and when: kids walking to school at 8 AM, riders heading to a bus stop, seniors going to a clinic.
  • Ask directly for a traffic study of the crossing, and ask how you will hear back.
  • If your city has a transportation or traffic safety commission, bring the same request to a public meeting.

Frequently asked questions

Who decides where crosswalks go?

The agency that owns the street. For most local streets that is the city or county traffic engineer, and for state highways it is the state department of transportation. They decide based on an engineering study and the MUTCD.

Why is there no crosswalk here?

Most often the location has not been studied, the counts did not show much crossing demand, or the street is wide and busy enough that paint alone would not be enough and a larger project is needed. A specific request through 311 or your transportation department is the way to get it looked at.

Do crosswalks need a traffic light?

No. A signal needs an engineering study, and the MUTCD lists nine signal warrants, including one for pedestrian volume. Meeting a warrant does not by itself require a signal. Many safe crossings use markings, refuge islands, or flashing beacons instead. See Not every crosswalk needs a traffic light.

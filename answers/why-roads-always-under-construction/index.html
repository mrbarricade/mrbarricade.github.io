Why are roads always under construction?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Because a street is a lot more than pavement. Water, sewer, gas, power, and telecom lines all share the space under it, each with its own owner and schedule, and the pavement itself needs regular repair before it fails. Add funding that often arrives in phases and weather limits on paving, and work on a busy corridor can feel endless.

What is under the street

Water, sewer, gas, electric, and telecom lines all run under the pavement. Each owner has its own maintenance needs, schedule, and approval process. Moving a curb even a few feet can mean moving a storm drain or a utility box. I wrote more about this in why some street projects take years.

Why they repave the same road

Often what looks like the same job twice is two different jobs. One crew may dig a trench to repair a pipe and patch it, and a later project resurfaces the whole street. The most common resurfacing method is mill and fill: the crew grinds off about an inch of old asphalt, leaves the surface scored and rough, and lays a fresh layer so the new asphalt bonds to the old. Skip the scoring and the new surface can break up.

Watch: Mill and fill is the most common asphalt repair method.

And when pavement keeps failing in the same spot, the asphalt is often not the real problem. As I put it in another video, asphalt fails because soil fails. A good repair fixes what is underneath.

Why projects take years

Before construction starts, a project has to answer questions you never see: where utilities are, who owns the land at the edge of the street, where rainwater will drain, how the work is funded, and what environmental and community review is needed. Many projects are funded by grants with their own rules and deadlines, and the money can arrive in phases. Those steps take time, and they are how a project gets better before it is built.

Why construction happens in summer

Asphalt needs warmth to be placed and compacted well. FHWA's sample paving specification sets minimum surface temperatures of 40 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit depending on how thick the layer is, and does not allow paving on a wet surface. Caltrans notes that warm mix asphalt, which is produced at lower temperatures, helps extend the paving season. Dry, warm months are simply when the work is most likely to last.

What you can do

  • Check your city's public works or transportation project pages for upcoming work on your street.
  • Report potholes and failing pavement through 311 with a photo and exact location.
  • If a street project is in planning, comment early. Repaving is also a natural moment to restripe for a bike lane or a better crosswalk, and FHWA publishes guidance on doing exactly that.

Frequently asked questions

Why do street projects take years?

Most of the time goes into questions answered before construction: utilities under the street, property lines, drainage, funding rules, and environmental and community review. Grants often come with their own schedules.

Why do they repave the same road?

Often it is two different jobs, like a utility trench patch followed by a full resurfacing. The common method, mill and fill, grinds off about an inch of old asphalt and lays a new layer. When pavement keeps failing in one spot, the soil underneath is often the cause.

Why does construction happen in summer?

Asphalt has to be placed and compacted warm and on a dry surface. FHWA's sample specification requires minimum surface temperatures of 40 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit depending on layer thickness.

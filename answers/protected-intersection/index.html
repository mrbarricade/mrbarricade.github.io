What is a protected intersection?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

A protected intersection keeps people on bikes physically separated from cars all the way through the corner. A corner island carries the separation into the intersection, the bike crossing is set back from the car lane so turning drivers see riders before they cross paths, and riders wait in a queue area past the crosswalk. NACTO cites a San Francisco design where 98 percent of drivers yielded to people on bikes and 100 percent to people walking.

The parts of a protected intersection

NACTO's guide Don't Give Up at the Intersection lays out the design:

  • Bikeway setback. The bike crossing is set back from the car lane. The example shows 10 feet, and 14 to 20 feet is preferred where practical. Setbacks over 20 feet may increase turn speeds.
  • Corner island. Small curb radii, usually 10 to 15 feet, discourage cars from turning faster than 10 mph. A mountable area can let large vehicles through.
  • Bike queue area. At least 6.5 feet deep, and 10 feet or more is desirable for cargo bikes and trailers.
  • Pedestrian islands. At least 6 feet wide, with 8 feet desired, so people walking cross shorter distances.

Why it works

The setback creates a space where a turning driver can stop and yield after starting the turn but before crossing the bike path. Riders are not forced to merge into traffic, and the setback makes them easier for turning drivers to see. NACTO notes that a study in New York found protected intersections had fewer vehicle and bike conflicts than even a dedicated turn lane with its own bike signal phase.

Speed is the other half. NACTO says driver yield rates fall as speeds rise, so turns should generally be slower than 10 mph.

It can start with paint

A protected intersection does not have to start with concrete. NACTO shows interim versions built with markings and flexible posts, with modular speed bumps at the corner in place of a concrete truck apron. More in quick build bike lanes.

Frequently asked questions

What is a protected intersection?

An intersection design that keeps bikes separated from cars through the corner, using a corner island, a set back bike crossing, a bike queue area, and pedestrian islands.

How slow should turns be at a protected intersection?

NACTO recommends turn speeds generally below 10 mph, achieved with corner curb radii of about 10 to 15 feet or less.

Can a protected intersection be built quickly?

Yes. NACTO shows interim designs using markings, flexible posts, and modular speed bumps in place of a concrete truck apron.

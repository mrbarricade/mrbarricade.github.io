How do you use a two stage turn box?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Ride straight through the intersection on green, stop in the marked box on the far side, turn your bike to face the cross street, and go when the cross street gets its green. That is a left turn in two stages, without merging across lanes of traffic. FHWA allowed the box in a 2017 interim approval, IA-20, for use at signalized intersections.

Step by step

  1. Stay in the bike lane and ride straight through on the green light.
  2. Pull into the turn box on the far side. It sits outside the path of moving traffic.
  3. Turn your bike to face the cross street. The bike symbol and arrow in the box show the direction.
  4. Wait for the green on the cross street, then ride straight ahead.

Where the bike lane is on the left side of the street, the same box can be used to turn right.

Why it exists

FHWA's memo explains that turning left from a curbside bike lane can be hard: on multilane roads, from physically separated lanes, or where rail tracks would have to be crossed at a shallow angle. The box gives people an alternative to merging across traffic, keeps them out of crosswalks and off sidewalks, and separates riders waiting to turn from riders going straight.

Design rules

  • Signalized intersections only.
  • Outlined with a solid white line, with a bike symbol and a turn arrow.
  • Placed beyond the cross street stop line and crosswalk, outside the paths of moving traffic.
  • No turn on red for any vehicle whose turn would pass through the box.
  • If the signal needs detection to give the cross street a green, the box must detect bikes waiting in it.
  • Signs are optional, unless the agency makes using the box mandatory.

What the experiments showed

FHWA reviewed 12 approved experiments. Riders made their two stage turns within or mostly within the box, and turning became more consistent. The available collision data did not show a negative safety effect: two sites saw fewer collisions and two saw an increase of one collision over a 19 month period.

An old idea

The design is not new. FHWA notes it appeared in California's 1972 Bikeway Planning Criteria and Guidelines and in AASHTO's 1974 Guide for Bicycle Routes.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to use a two stage turn box?

Only where signs say it is mandatory. FHWA requires regulatory signs where an agency makes the box mandatory. Otherwise, check your state's vehicle code for how people on bikes may turn left.

Can cars turn right on red across a two stage turn box?

No. FHWA's interim approval requires NO TURN ON RED signs where turning vehicles would pass through the box.

Will the light change if I wait in the box?

It should. Where detection is needed to call a green for the cross street, FHWA requires passive bike detection in the box.

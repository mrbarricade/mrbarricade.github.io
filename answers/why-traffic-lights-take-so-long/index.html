Why do traffic lights take so long?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Most traffic lights are running a timing plan, not reacting to you alone. Every direction, including people walking, gets a minimum amount of green, every change includes yellow and clearance time, and many signals stay in step with their neighbors. When a sensor fails or a plan was built for rush hour, you can end up waiting at an empty intersection.

Fixed timing versus sensors

Signals run in one of two basic ways. Pretimed signals repeat the same sequence on a set schedule, and FHWA notes that many agencies still use pretimed controllers. Actuated signals use detectors to decide how long each direction gets.

An actuated signal still has rules. FHWA describes five basic settings for each movement: a minimum green, an extension that keeps the green going while cars keep arriving, a maximum green, a yellow, and a red. Each phase must have a minimum green so a stopped vehicle has time to get moving before yellow appears.

How the sensors work

The most common sensor is the inductive loop: wire laid in a shallow slot cut into the pavement, connected to electronics in the signal cabinet. When a vehicle stops over it, the controller gets a call. FHWA's detector handbook says the loop has been the most utilized sensor in traffic management since it was introduced in the early 1960s.

Why it turns red when no one is coming

  • Coordination. Signals along a corridor often share a cycle length, an offset, and a split so traffic can flow through several lights. Your light may be holding its place in that pattern.
  • People walking. If someone pushed the button, the signal has to time a walk interval plus clearance. The MUTCD sizes clearance for a walking speed of 3.5 feet per second, so a wide street needs a long walk phase.
  • Broken detection. When a loop fails, a common fix is to put that movement on maximum recall, which gives it full green every cycle. FHWA notes this can increase intersection delay by 50 percent or more.

The safety intervals you never notice

The MUTCD recommends a yellow change interval of 3 to 6 seconds, with the longer yellows on faster approaches, and a red clearance interval that generally should not exceed 6 seconds. Those few seconds, repeated for every movement, add up. They are also there so the person running the yellow does not meet the person starting on green.

What you can do

  • Report a light that seems stuck or slow through 311 or your city's transportation department. Give the intersection, your direction of travel, and the time of day.
  • If the signal never seems to see your car or bike, say so. That points staff to a detection problem.
  • Ask whether the intersection is part of a coordinated corridor and when its timing was last reviewed.

Frequently asked questions

How do traffic light sensors work?

The most common sensor is an inductive loop, a coil of wire set in a slot in the pavement. When a vehicle stops over it, the detector sends a call to the signal controller, which decides when to serve that direction.

Why do lights turn red with no one coming?

The signal may be coordinated with nearby lights, serving a pedestrian who pushed the button, or running on a fallback setting because a detector failed. FHWA notes a failed detector placed on maximum recall can raise delay by 50 percent or more.

What is signal timing?

Signal timing is the set of rules that decides the order and length of each green, yellow, and red, plus walk and clearance times. For actuated signals that includes the minimum green, extension, and maximum green, and for coordinated corridors the cycle length, offset, and split.

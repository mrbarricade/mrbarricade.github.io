Why are bike lanes green?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Green pavement marks where people on bikes are expected to ride and where bikes and cars may cross paths, such as at intersections, driveways, and turn lanes. FHWA approved green for this use in an April 2011 interim approval, known as IA-14, after experiments found riders positioned themselves more accurately and drivers said it made them more aware of where bikes would be.

How green was tested

Before 2011, FHWA had approved green pavement experiments for agencies including New York, Chicago, Portland, San Francisco, Austin, and the Vermont and Minnesota transportation departments. FHWA reviewed the results and found them satisfactorily successful. Riders positioned themselves more accurately across intersections and conflict areas, riders said they felt safer, and drivers said the green made them more aware that bikes might be present. FHWA noted no notable negative operational effects.

Why not blue or red?

FHWA's memo gives the reasons directly. Blue could not be used because it is already the color of the accessible parking symbol and the lines next to accessible parking spaces. Red had not been approved for any bike experiments because it was being tested for a different use. For the ten years before the approval, green was the only color FHWA had approved for bike facility experiments.

Where green can go

  • Along all or part of a bike lane.
  • As a rectangle behind the bike symbol, to make the markings easier to see.
  • Between the dotted lines that carry a bike lane across an intersection, driveway, or ramp, or across the start of a turn lane where drivers must cross the bike lane. Here the green can be dotted to match the lines.

What green cannot do

Green is a supplement. Under IA-14 it cannot replace the white lane line, the bike symbol and arrow markings, or the dotted lines through a conflict area. Using it is optional for agencies. FHWA also says agencies should choose marking materials that minimize loss of traction for people on bikes.

As an engineer, I read green as a warning label: it is used most where paths cross, which is where you should look twice.

Frequently asked questions

What do dashed green bike lanes mean?

Dashed green usually fills the space between dotted lines that carry a bike lane through an intersection, driveway, ramp, or the start of a turn lane. It marks an area where drivers may cross the bike lane, so both sides should watch for each other.

Are green bike lanes required?

No. FHWA's 2011 interim approval made green optional. Agencies that use it must still provide the standard white line and bike markings.

Why are bike lanes not blue?

FHWA ruled out blue because it is already used for the accessible parking symbol and for the lines next to accessible parking spaces.

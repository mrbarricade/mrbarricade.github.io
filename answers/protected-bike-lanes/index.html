What is a protected bike lane, and are they safer?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

A protected bike lane, also called a separated bike lane, has a physical barrier such as a curb, flexible posts, or a planted strip between people biking and moving traffic. Yes, they are safer: FHWA reports that converting a regular or painted buffer bike lane to a separated lane with flexible posts can reduce bicycle and vehicle crashes by up to 53 percent.

Paint, buffer, or protection

A conventional bike lane is a painted stripe. A buffered lane adds a painted gap between bikes and cars. A separated, or protected, lane adds vertical elements in that gap. FHWA lists flexible delineator posts, curbs, and vegetation as examples, and recommends separated lanes on roads with higher vehicle volumes and speeds, such as arterials.

The safety case

FHWA lists bicycle lanes as a Proven Safety Countermeasure. It reports that most fatal and serious injury bicycle crashes happen away from intersections, and nearly one third happen when a driver is overtaking someone on a bike. Separation targets exactly that crash.

  • Converting a traditional or flush buffered lane to a separated lane with flexible posts: up to 53 percent fewer bicycle and vehicle crashes.
  • Adding bike lanes on urban four lane undivided collectors and local roads: up to 49 percent fewer total crashes.
  • Adding bike lanes on urban two lane undivided collectors and local roads: up to 30 percent fewer total crashes.

Why bike lanes are green

The MUTCD allows green colored pavement to make bike lanes and conflict areas more visible, for example where drivers cross a bike lane to turn or merge. Green marks the spots where people driving should expect people biking.

Do bike lanes cause traffic?

FHWA reports that studies found roads did not see an increase in crashes or congestion when travel lanes were narrowed to add a bike lane, and that bike lanes increase ridership and may help agencies manage roadway capacity.

The intersection is where it counts

A protected lane that disappears at the corner leaves people exposed right where turning conflicts happen. The NACTO Urban Bikeway Design Guide, Third Edition, published in 2025, has a whole section titled "Don't Give Up at the Intersection." A protected intersection keeps the separation through the corner with a few key moves, which I walk through in this video:

Watch: Protected Intersection Best Practices

  • The bike lane jogs away from traffic before the crossing, so turning drivers meet people biking at close to a right angle, where they can see each other.
  • Corner islands give people biking a protected place to wait.
  • Bike stop bars sit ahead of where cars wait, so riders are visible.
  • Tight corners, sometimes with a truck apron, keep turning speeds down.

What you can do

  • Ask your city's transportation department whether it has a bike plan, and where your street is in it.
  • If a painted lane feels unsafe, report it through 311 and ask whether separation is possible, especially at the next repaving.
  • Show up early when a corridor project starts. That is when protection at intersections gets decided.

Frequently asked questions

Why are bike lanes green?

The MUTCD allows green colored pavement to increase the visibility of bike lanes and of areas where bikes and other traffic cross, weave, or merge. It tells drivers where to expect people on bikes.

Do bike lanes cause traffic?

FHWA reports that studies found no increase in crashes or congestion when travel lanes were narrowed to add a bike lane, and that bike lanes increase ridership.

What does a protected intersection look like?

The bike lane stays separated through the corner: it jogs away from traffic before the crosswalk, corner islands protect people waiting on bikes, bike stop bars sit ahead of car stop bars, and tight corners slow turning drivers.

Where do protected bike lanes make the most sense?

FHWA recommends separated bike lanes on roads with higher vehicle volumes and speeds, such as arterials.

Are roundabouts safer than intersections?

By Vignesh Swaminathan, PE (Mr. Barricade)

Yes, in most locations. FHWA reports that converting a two way stop intersection to a roundabout reduces fatal and injury crashes by 82 percent, and converting a signalized intersection reduces them by 78 percent. Roundabouts work because they force lower speeds and reduce the places where paths cross.

Why the design is safer

FHWA describes three features that do the work: curved, channelized approaches that slow vehicles down, yield control at entry so drivers give way to traffic already circulating, and counterclockwise flow around a central island that minimizes conflict points. Lower speeds and fewer conflicts mean the crashes that do happen are far less likely to injure or kill someone.

The numbers come from the Highway Safety Manual, and FHWA lists the roundabout as a Proven Safety Countermeasure: an 82 percent reduction in fatal and injury crashes when replacing a two way stop, and 78 percent when replacing a signal.

What about traffic?

FHWA notes roundabouts can reduce delay and queuing compared with other intersection types, even while calming traffic. The FHWA traffic calming guide says a multilane modern roundabout can be effective at daily entering volumes up to 60,000 vehicles.

How to drive a roundabout

  • Slow down on the approach and watch for people in the crosswalk before the yield line.
  • Yield to traffic already in the circle, then enter when there is a gap.
  • Travel counterclockwise and do not stop inside the circle unless you must.
  • In a multilane roundabout, pick your lane before you enter, based on the signs and arrows.

How people walking cross

Crosswalks sit on each approach, set back from the circle, often with a raised island in the middle so you cross one direction of traffic at a time. FHWA's guidance on refuge islands shows this layout, with the crosswalk more than a vehicle length from the roundabout. At busier approaches, agencies can add rectangular rapid flashing beacons, which FHWA allows at the approach or exit of a roundabout.

Not a fit everywhere

A full roundabout needs room. FHWA's traffic calming guide says it is typically appropriate where arterial streets meet each other or meet collectors, and that a mini roundabout or traffic circle fits smaller neighborhood intersections. Right of way, utilities, and the vehicles that need to get through all shape the decision.

What you can do

If an intersection near you has a pattern of serious crashes, ask your city's transportation department through 311 whether a roundabout was considered in its intersection evaluation, and request a traffic study if none has been done.

Frequently asked questions

Why are roundabouts better?

They slow everyone down and reduce conflict points. FHWA reports 82 percent fewer fatal and injury crashes when replacing a two way stop and 78 percent fewer when replacing a signal, and they can reduce delay and queuing.

How do you drive a roundabout?

Slow down, yield to people in the crosswalk and to vehicles already circulating, enter when there is a gap, and travel counterclockwise to your exit. In multilane roundabouts, choose your lane before entering.

How do pedestrians cross a roundabout?

At marked crosswalks on each approach, set back from the circle, usually with a refuge island so you cross one direction of traffic at a time. Busier crossings can have flashing beacons.
